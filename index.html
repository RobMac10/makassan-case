Makassan trepangers in Arnhem Land, 1700s–1907

Why did the Makassan voyages to Arnhem Land end, and who decided?

What is agreed

From at least the 1700s until 1907, fleets of boats called praus sailed from Makassar, on the island of Sulawesi (now in Indonesia), to the coast of Arnhem Land, which the visitors called Marege’. They came with the north-west monsoon about December and left about April.

They came for trepang, also called sea cucumber or bêche-de-mer, which they gathered, boiled and dried, and which was sold on to buyers in China.

On 17 February 1803 Matthew Flinders, commanding HMS Investigator, met six praus at the islands he named the English Company’s Islands. Their leader was named Pobassoo. Flinders’ Malay cook interpreted.

The visitors met, traded and worked with Yolŋu people. They brought metal tools, cloth, tobacco and words still used today. They did not settle in Arnhem Land.

From 1863 the Northern Territory was governed by South Australia. In the 1880s the South Australian government began to charge the Makassan fleets customs duties and a licence fee. The Commonwealth took over the Northern Territory on 1 January 1911.

In 1906 the government decided to stop issuing licences to the Makassan trepangers. The last prau from Makassar came to Arnhem Land in 1907. Public sources disagree about which government made the decision, and why.

Green on the printed page marks what can be agreed. Amber, used in Super Sleuths, marks what is still disputed or was added later.

Three witnesses

Matthew Flinders, reporting Pobassoo

Commander of HMS Investigator · Met the praus at the English Company’s Islands, 17–19 February 1803 · A Voyage to Terra Australis, 1814 · Died 1814

An English navigator who wrote down what a Makassan captain told him, through an interpreter.

Source. Matthew Flinders, A Voyage to Terra Australis (London, 1814), vol. 2, book 2, chapter 9 (text on Wikisource). Portrait by Toussaint Antoine de Chazal, about 1806–07, Wikimedia Commons (Google Art Project), public domain.

What they said. “The chief of the six prows was a short, elderly man, named Pobassoo.” “Pobassoo had made six or seven voyages from Macassar to this coast, within the preceding twenty years, and he was one of the first who came.” The trepang “is carried to Timor, and sold to the Chinese, who meet them there.” “They sometimes had skirmishes with the native inhabitants of the coast.”

Could they really have seen it? Flinders was there, and he questioned the captains for two days. But almost everything he wrote about the fleet came from Pobassoo, through Flinders’ cook. He saw one small part of the coast in one season. He could not see the earlier voyages, or the end of the trade a century later.

Why might they say it? To report a trade that a British colony might one day want for itself. He wrote that a colony on the east coast “might perhaps derive considerable advantage from the trepang.” Students can ask what Pobassoo wanted Flinders to know, and what Flinders wanted his readers in London to think.

Alfred Searcy

Sub-Collector of Customs at Port Darwin for fourteen years, from the 1880s · In Australian Tropics, first published 1907

The South Australian customs officer who sailed along the coast to tell the Makassan captains they must now pay.

Source. Alfred Searcy, In Australian Tropics (London: George Robertson, first published 1907; quoted from the third edition, 1909), pp. 12–13, text on the Internet Archive. No period portrait of Searcy is used here. The image is a silhouette, not a likeness.

What they said. “Now that there was some chance of Europeans following suit, and with the idea of encouraging local trading on the coast, it was decided that the time had come for the Malays to be placed on an equal footing with the local people, and to pay something towards the revenue of the country, to which hitherto they had not contributed a penny.” “I made preparations for my first voyage down the coast to warn the masters of the proas that in future a licence would have to be paid, and also duty on the stores used on the coast.”

Could they really have seen it? He was the official who collected the money, and he sailed the coast himself from 1884. He could see the licences and the fleets. He was writing a lively memoir years later, and he does not say who in Adelaide made the decision. He left the Territory before the trade ended in 1906–07.

Why might they say it? To explain and defend his own work, and to entertain readers at home. His claim that the visitors had taken “millions of pounds’ worth” from the coast is his own estimate, not a figure from the customs books. Students can ask how a tax collector’s view of the trade might differ from a Makassan captain’s, or a Yolŋu family’s.

National Museum of Australia

National museum, Canberra · Defining Moments page, updated 5 August 2024 · Curator’s public talk, 9 July 2008

A national museum that has explained the end of the trade in two public texts, in two different ways.

Source. National Museum of Australia, “Trade with the Makasar,” Defining Moments, nma.gov.au (updated 5 August 2024); and the transcript of curator Alison Mercieca’s talk “From Makassar to Marege to the Museum,” 9 July 2008, nma.gov.au. Quoted from both pages. The image is a document silhouette, not a facsimile.

What they said. The Defining Moments page: “After 1901 the newly formed Australian Government banned trepangers from Makassar in order to protect Australia’s ‘territorial integrity’ and to encourage a local trepang industry. In 1907 the last prau from Makassar visited Arnhem Land.” The 2008 talk: “The industry had been in decline since the 1880s when the South Australian government, who oversaw the affairs of the Northern Territory at the time, began to charge the Macassans customs duties and then a licence fee.” “In 1906 the government decided to ban Macassan trepang fishing, the main reason being to protect and encourage the local industry.”

Could they really have seen it? The museum was not there. It summarises historians’ research for the public. Its two texts were written sixteen years apart, by different writers, for different purposes.

Why might they say it? To give the public a short, clear account. A short account has to choose one cause and one decision-maker. Students can ask why one page names the Australian Government and the other names no government at all for the 1906 decision.

Why it still matters

Long before 1788, the north coast was part of a trading world that reached Makassar and China. The file is open because the end of that world was decided on paper, far from the beaches, and public accounts still disagree about whose paper it was. Who gets to decide who may come to a coast, and for whose benefit, is not only a question about 1906.

Super Sleuths

The Makassan voyages reached Yolŋu Country. This file uses published public records only, and does not retell Yolŋu stories or songs. Amber marks what is still disputed, or what later writers added.

1. The notebook is written for this file

The typed page is a constructed student voice in front of a museum case. The narrator is not a historical person. The facts in it (December and April, the camps, the 1906 disagreement) come from the agreed file. “Maybe a hundred and fifty years” is the narrator’s rough sum, not a source.

Question. Which words in the notebook show the narrator is unsure, and is that a weakness or a strength?

2. When did it begin?

The National Museum says “from at least 1700.” Historians have long placed the first voyages in the first half of the 1700s. Pobassoo told Flinders in 1803 that he was “one of the first who came,” within the past twenty years, and one historian has reconsidered a start date of about 1780 on that basis.

Question. How much weight can one captain’s memory, written down by someone else through an interpreter, carry?

3. Who signed?

South Australia governed the Northern Territory until 1 January 1911. The 2008 museum talk says “the government” decided in 1906. The museum’s later page says “the newly formed Australian Government.” A 2024 article in Australian Historical Studies, “The Macassan–Aboriginal relationship and the Commonwealth of Australia 1901–1906,” studies how the two governments dealt with the question.

Question. What document would you need to see to settle who made the 1906 decision?

4. Revenue, protection, or the border?

Searcy gives revenue and “encouraging local trading.” The 2008 talk gives protecting “the local industry.” The Defining Moments page adds “territorial integrity.” These are three different reasons, and they can all be true at once.

Question. Rank the three reasons from best supported to least supported by the witnesses in this file.

5. The last voyage

The 2008 talk says only four praus came in the 1905–06 season, one captained by Using Daeng Rangka, and that in 1907 he sailed again to check whether the ban was true. It was. He has an entry in the Australian Dictionary of Biography.

Question. Why might a captain sail all that way to check a rule, instead of believing it?

6. Sure, maybe, or a guess?

Sure: praus sailed from Makassar to Arnhem Land for trepang for well over a century; Flinders met Pobassoo in 1803; South Australia charged duties and licences from the 1880s; licences stopped in 1906 and the last prau came in 1907. Maybe: which government made the final decision, and its main reason. A guess: what Yolŋu families thought of the ban. The opener is not a source.

Question. Write a finding of no more than five sentences, and mark each sentence sure, maybe, or a guess.

Country, and care

The Makassan voyages are part of Yolŋu history, and Yolŋu communities hold songs, ceremonies and stories about them. None of these are retold here, and no Yolŋu art is reproduced. Flinders records “skirmishes” and that Pobassoo had been speared; violence on both sides is part of the record and is not described further. Period words such as “Malays” and “natives” appear only inside short quotations. Teachers wishing to add Yolŋu perspectives should seek community-approved resources and follow the AIATSIS Code of Ethics.

Source conflicts, flagged

Start date: “from at least 1700” (NMA) against Pobassoo’s twenty years (Flinders). Decision-maker in 1906: the Australian Government (NMA Defining Moments) against “the government” in a talk that names South Australia as the Territory’s administrator (NMA, 2008). The 2024 Australian Historical Studies article should be read by teachers before choosing a side. Spellings: Makassar for the place and Makasar for the people (NMA usage); Macassan in older writing.
